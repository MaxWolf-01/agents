# What's manual, what's unattended, and why

- **Grilling is where alignment happens on fog**: an intent too unclear to brief is grilled with you in the loop, non-negotiable, until the ticket can be written and a sensible first version built; `/mx:grilling` says what its questions lean to. Everything downstream trades on the shared understanding built there. An intent with one sensible shape skips it: you rule on the build, since a call is judged best in front of the concrete thing, and nothing merges before you do. A call the build rests on that you may want to set is named when it starts; answer it while it runs, or when the landing shows it to you. External inputs (a meeting transcript, a client brief, a bug report) are drafted like any intent: the foggy ones are grilled through their unstated assumptions.
- **Plan in one window, respect the smart zone.** Grilling (which writes the ticket) → the cut into child tickets stays in one unbroken context window; but reasoning degrades noticeably from roughly 30% of the window used, regardless of advertised size. Approaching the limit mid-planning → end the session with the frontier open (the sharp questions become child tickets that need you) or handoff to a fresh thread; don't push on degraded.
- **The design is reviewed as it is written.** A wrong line of code is one wrong line; a wrong line in the design becomes hundreds of them, so the ticket is where a look pays most. Grilling writes it round by round and you review each round as it lands, the ticket in front of the figures that explain it, both linked from the round's turn on the session page (`/mx:grilling` has the mechanics); writing it down is itself a design step. A ticket assembled across sessions gets one whole-document read before it is cut, for drift between sessions.
- **The ticket breakdown is the agent's.** How a ticket is cut is the agent's call: it cuts a child ticket only where that child's worker needs little of the others' context, files the children `open`, checks their shape itself (vertical, independent), and the workers start. The rest stays one ticket, one worker building its slices as commits and handing off to a fresh worker when its context passes its mark (400k tokens by default). You judge the built tree at its close-out, which is where a slice too coarse to demo shows up anyway.
- **Proposed tickets wait for your triage.** What an agent files on its own reading (a worker's friction, a review finding, a punt) is `proposed` and stays off the frontier, so the pile gets read together and a pattern across several tickets shows. You start a triage when you want one (`/mx:triage`): the pile is grouped by shared cause, the agent applies the bookkeeping (drops, merges, promotions, priorities) and reports it in one turn, and you are asked only the design calls, each shown with something to look at. Your own "build this" makes a ticket open at once.
- **The build runs while you are away.** It starts when the interview has no question left for you; every call you have not ruled on stays marked in its ticket, or is an anchored assumption on the review page where a worker made it. A build carrying calls you have not made that change what you asked for waits for your ruling (accept, amend, redo or reject), and you are shown those calls alone, each with the artefact that makes it visible; one carrying none merges into its parent ticket's branch on your standing yes, and into the trunk only on your word: a ship, merge or accept you give once you have seen it, or up front for a change you named yourself. Either way the landing comes with a show of what was built, for whenever you look, unless the diff alone makes the change plain at a glance. Nothing merges above a parent ticket's branch before you rule. You rule a parent ticket whole, with its children, at its close-out: a child merges into the parent's branch once the orchestrator has read it, and its siblings build on it unruled. So a shape the children build on is settled before the cut; one a child raises anyway comes to you as a question on its ticket, and the children that would build on it wait for your answer.
- **Implementation is the AFK part.** Day shift plans and cuts the tickets; night shift works the frontier, fresh context per ticket.
- **QA is where you impose taste, per landed slice.** Manual, deliberately: automate the idea, the planning, *and* the QA and you get slop. Every ticket is a tracer bullet, drivable the moment it lands: the worker builds, tests and reports, and the landing hands you its review page and, unless the diff alone makes the change plain at a glance, one show of what was built, made by a fork of the session that lands it (`/mx:show`). The show opens with the intent and how the parts fit, goes a level lower only where the one above needs it, and a script it leaves that runs the thing gets a button on the ticket's board row; you drive what you want to on your own machine while the remaining frontier keeps running (`dispatch ps` lists every worker on every host, `dispatch peek <slug>` prints one's pane and `dispatch attach <slug>` takes it). Your findings become new tickets with blocking edges, which the board absorbs, and a decision you reopen goes back to grilling.
- **Reviews run in fresh context.** A reviewer sharing the implementer's window reviews in the dumb zone; a worker closes with code-review in clean context for a reason.
- **A session answers on its page.** Every session you sit at has a page from its first reply, one browser page per session with what waits on you at the top (questions to decide, to-dos, artefacts to look at, one card at a time, your answers staged and sent as one message) and the turns below, newest first, each linking the artefacts it made. A turn that delivers something (an answer, an artefact, a question, a landing) writes it there as a turn record; a turn that only reports where it stands ends on a line or two in the chat, which the page shows as a chat turn. A turn that writes a record ends without a chat message of its own, so each answer is written once; what waits on you lives in the record, its questions and to-dos staying at the page's top until a later record clears them. As the turn ends the Stop hook opens every artefact the turn linked in your browser, and the page with them the first time, or on every turn where the container hub runs (see "The session page, in the hub tab" in the [README](../README.md)). In the terminal it shows a message of its own in the agent's place: the record's headline, the link to the page, and each question and to-do waiting on you by its headline, the ones asked since your last message marked new. When the session ends, a hook commits its records (not the page, which re-renders from them) to the agent repo in a commit of their own, so what a session reported and asked outlives it. The agent writes plain-text turn records and hands every artefact to a subagent or a fork, so no HTML and no render loop fills its context (`mx/skills/session-page/RULES.md`, which `claude/CLAUDE.md` imports into every session).
- **A session page's prose has a reviewer too.** A turn that writes a record for its session page gets one light review the moment the record is written: a model reads it against the chat-scoped rules of `/mx:writing-for-humans`'s catalogue, seeing what you have seen on the page, and hands a few findings back while the turn still runs, so the session revises the record before the turn ends. It fails open, stays out of a dispatched worker's session, and logs every decision; `mx/skills/session-page/turn_review.py` says where the log goes.
- **Feedback loops are the ceiling.** Agent output quality tracks the quality of the repo's tests and typechecks. Bad output → improve the loops, not the prompt (`/mx:improve-codebase-architecture`; deep modules: design the interface, delegate the implementation).
- **A test earns its place, and the full-path checks run on purpose.** A test holds a behaviour you rely on, pins a bug that reached you, or states an invariant of a seam; one doing none of those is deleted, and one that flakes is fixed or deleted the day it flakes (`/mx:testing`). `make test` is the fast suite every change runs; the full-path checks drive the real thing (a browser on the page, tmux, a paid API) and run when work reaching their path lands, and before a release. Property tests come with architecture work, where stating a seam's invariant is how the seam gets clear; a ticket's properties are prose each review checks the diff against. What a tree's close-out suite and review propose is filed `proposed`, like anything an agent files on its own reading, and waits for your triage.
- **Done work gets deleted** (`git rm`). Closed tickets left in the tree are doc rot steering future agents wrong; git history keeps them.
